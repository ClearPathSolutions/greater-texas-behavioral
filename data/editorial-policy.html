Who Creates and Reviews Our Content

Our content is created by writers and editors who specialize in behavioral health, working alongside our clinical team.

Writers and editors research each topic, draft the content and edit it for clarity, accuracy and tone.

Clinical reviewers are licensed professionals, such as physicians, psychiatrists, nurses, therapists and counselors, who review content that makes medical or clinical claims.

Every article shows who wrote it and, when clinically reviewed, the reviewer's name, credentials and review date. Each name links to a bio describing that person's qualifications and experience.

Our Editorial Process

Every piece of content moves through the same six stages before it reaches our readers.

  1. Topic selection. We choose topics based on the questions patients and families ask our admissions team, current research and trends in substance use and mental health.
  2. Research. Writers gather information from the high-quality sources described below and note every source used.
  3. Writing. Content is written in plain language, at a level most adults can read comfortably, without jargon or sensational claims.
  4. Editorial review. An editor checks accuracy, sourcing, tone, readability and our stigma-free language standards. Headlines must describe the content honestly.
  5. Clinical review. Content that discusses symptoms, withdrawal, medications, treatment approaches or health risks is reviewed by a licensed clinician before publishing.
  6. Publishing and upkeep. Each page is scheduled for a periodic review so it stays current.

Sources and Citations

We build our content on trustworthy, current evidence. Preferred sources include:

  • Federal health agencies such as SAMHSA, the National Institute on Drug Abuse (NIDA), the National Institute on Alcohol Abuse and Alcoholism (NIAAA), the National Institute of Mental Health (NIMH) and the CDC
  • Peer-reviewed medical and scientific journals
  • Professional bodies and clinical guidelines, including the American Society of Addiction Medicine (ASAM) and the American Psychiatric Association
  • Our own licensed clinicians and other recognized experts in addiction medicine and mental health

We avoid citing competitor treatment websites, unsourced blogs, forums or commercial sites as evidence. Statistics come from the most recent data available, and we cite the year the data was collected.

Articles include a source list linking to the original material. Direct quotes are attributed by name and are never altered in a way that changes their meaning. We do not fabricate quotes, sources, statistics or credentials.

Clinical Accuracy Standards

Our clinical reviewers check that content:

  • Reflects current, evidence-based practice and recognized clinical guidelines
  • Describes withdrawal, detox and medication topics safely, and never encourages anyone to detox or change medication without medical supervision
  • Presents treatment options fairly, including the limits of what any program can promise
  • Makes no guarantees about outcomes, recovery timelines or "cure" rates
  • Describes our own services, levels of care and insurance acceptance accurately

If a reviewer flags a concern, the content is revised and reviewed again before it goes live.

Content Updates and Corrections

Medical knowledge changes, and so does our content. We review clinical articles at least every 12 months, and sooner when new research, guidelines, drug trends or laws make a page outdated. Each article shows its last reviewed or updated date.

When we make a factual error, we correct it promptly. Significant corrections are noted on the page with the date of the change. Typos and formatting fixes are made without a note.

If you see something inaccurate, outdated or unclear, please tell us at {{EDITORIAL_EMAIL}}. We review every report.

Person-First, Stigma-Free Language

Shame keeps people from asking for help, and language plays a part in that. We use person-first, medically accurate terms and avoid labels that define someone by their illness.

We useInstead of
Person with a substance use disorderAddict, junkie, abuser
Substance use / misuseDrug abuse (except in formal diagnostic or legal names)
In recovery, not currently usingClean
Returned to use, recurrenceRelapsed as failure, fell off the wagon
Medication for addiction treatmentReplacement drug, trading one drug for another
Died by suicideCommitted suicide

We respect how people in recovery describe themselves. When someone sharing their story uses a different term for their own experience, we may keep their words in direct quotes.

We also write for a diverse audience, including people of every age, background, gender identity, sexual orientation, faith and profession, and families supporting a loved one.
